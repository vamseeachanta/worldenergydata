Offshore mega-project cost inflation over time

The Deckhand Chronicles is AceEngineer's newsletter. We help build AI into everyday engineering work.

What we did

A mega project fixes its budget at sanction, then spends it over several years while the prices of rigs, steel, equipment and engineering move. Public price indexes are set against consumer prices from 2000 to 2025. The final costs of existing offshore projects are set against their sanction estimates, from public records. This article continues our series on costing toward mega projects, which grows with each project read.

Why it matters

  • Study how far offshore cost indexes have outrun consumer prices, and how far each boom and bust moved them.
  • Check an estimate against the escalation met in each sanction year, and against what earlier projects finally cost.
  • Rerun the comparison with any cost index, spend profile or execution window.
  • Prompt: rebuild the index comparison, escalation range and project view from the public files, in worldenergydata on GitHub.

What is inside

The body gives the long-run rise, the three cycles, a check against a licensed sector index, the escalation range by sanction year and a first project-level view. Appendix A holds the data, method and tables. Appendix B holds the prompt.

Offshore cost indexes rose faster than consumer prices

Seven public US cost indexes, rebased to their 2000 means, are compared with consumer prices from 2000 to 2025. The yearly growth of each above consumer prices is shown in Figure 1.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • Consumer prices rose 87.0 %, or 2.53 % a year [1].
  • The cost of drilling oil and gas wells rose 189.4 % and steel pipe and tube 252.9 % [2][6]: 1.76 % and 2.57 % a year above consumer prices.
  • Engineering services, ship building and support activities kept pace with consumer prices, within 0.13 % a year of them [3][7][8].
  • The capital blend, seven cost indexes weighted as for an offshore development, rose 3.35 % a year, 0.79 % a year above consumer prices.

Three cycles moved costs by tens of percent

The monthly indexes against consumer prices, with the three cycles shaded, are shown in Figure 2.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • 2004 to 2008: the capital blend rose 63.9 % from 2003 to 2008, 40.1 % above consumer prices; drilling rose 139.0 % [2].
  • 2014 to 2016: the blend fell 12.2 %; drilling fell 33.9 % from March 2014 to November 2016 [2].
  • 2021 to 2023: the blend rose 26.0 % from 2020 to 2023, 7.0 % above consumer prices; steel mill products rose 153.2 % from August 2020 to December 2021 [5].
  • 2026, January to October, above the 2025 means: the blend 4.0 %, steel mill products 19.6 %, Brent 39.4 % [5][9].

Our hypothesis: the oil price sets the swing in drilling and steel costs, with drilling about a year behind (correlation 0.56 on the previous year's Brent change [9]). Engineering and yard fabrication costs follow it more weakly and keep pace with consumer prices over the long run.

Public indexes understate the 2008 to 2012 peak

The S&P Global (formerly IHS) Upstream Capital Costs Index is licensed; its values are public only where the press quoted them [10]–[17], [19]–[22]. The quoted values beside the two public blends are shown in Figure 3.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • The quoted index reached 230 in the third quarter of 2008 [11]; the capital blend stood at 184.4 in that quarter.
  • In the 7 quoted values from 2008 to 2012, the quoted index sits 20.5 % to 24.7 % above the capital blend.
  • In the 5 quoted values of 2006, 2019 and 2020, it sits 0.8 % to 4.2 % below.
  • No quoted value was found for 2014 to 2018 or after 2020; the 2013 value is set aside (Appendix A).

Our hypothesis: in a boom, global yard, vessel and subsea equipment markets add cost that US producer price indexes do not see. An escalation built on them alone would then understate a boom.

A longer execution window widens the escalation range

For each sanction year, the estimate is set in that year's money and spent over 5 or 8 years on a bell-shaped profile. The escalation above consumer prices each project would have met is shown in Figure 4.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • 5-year window, sanction years 1997 to 2021: P50 0.7 % above consumer prices, P90 11.8 %.
  • 8-year window, 22 overlapping sanction years from 1997 to 2018: P50 0.7 %, P90 22.8 %.
  • The worst sanction year is 2003: 29.7 % above consumer prices over 8 years, 44.2 % in all; the best is 2014, at −11.8 %.
  • On a P50 estimate, the escalation contingency to P90 is 11.1 percentage points for 5 years and 22.1 for 8 years. Both come on top of the allowance for consumer prices.

Public records give a first view of project cost

Project costs are read from public records: the estimate at sanction, valued contract awards and dated statements to the final figure [24]. Each final figure over its sanction estimate is shown in Figure 5, beside the capital blend.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • Of 110 public contract awards across 29 projects, 59 carry a value, 33 of them from the trade press.
  • Capital awards with a value cover a median 12.1 % of the sanction estimate over 28 projects, from 0 % to 61.9 % (Martin Linge).
  • 12 projects publish a sanction estimate and a final figure in one currency; for 10 the change can be computed, from 32.5 % below (Johan Sverdrup Phase 1, sanctioned 2015) to 100.0 % above (Martin Linge, 2012). 9 of the 10 rest on an ASSUMED figure.
  • Of these, 4 of the 6 sanctioned from 2009 to 2013 finished above their estimate, and the 2 sanctioned in 2015 below it; over those years the capital blend moved −5.0 % to +11.3 %.

Method

  • Annual value: the calendar-year mean of the monthly values. Rebased index: the annual value over its 2000 value, times 100.
  • Growth above consumer prices: the rebased index over the rebased consumer price index. Growth per year is compounded from 2000 to 2025.
  • Capital blend: the weighted mean of seven rebased cost indexes, with the weights of Table A2. The weights are our assumption for the capital of a deepwater development; they are not measured cost shares.
  • Cycles: the change in annual means from 2003 to 2008, 2014 to 2016 and 2020 to 2023 (Table A3). The turning months in the body are the highest and lowest monthly values searched from 2012 to 2018 for drilling and from 2020 to 2024 for steel mill products.
  • Escalation over a window: for sanction year s and a window of N years, the spend share of year s + k is proportional to sin(π(k + 0.5)/N); escalation is the spend-weighted index over its sanction-year value, less one. Percentiles interpolate linearly across the sanction years. The 5-year and 8-year windows and the bell-shaped spend profile are our assumptions; Table A4 checks an even spend.
  • Correlation: Pearson correlation of the yearly percentage changes of the annual means.

Project costs

A project's cost is assembled from three kinds of public record, and each value is used as its source states it:

  • Sources that count: the operator's or a partner's release, annual report, investor presentation or filing; a regulator's document; and a contractor's own release, for the figure each states. A value read in the trade press, or in a later report that quotes the operator, and a value derived rather than stated, are used only where no such source was found. Each is marked † and is ASSUMED - to be confirmed by 11 Dec 2026 against the operator's or the regulator's own document.
  • Estimate at sanction: the gross capital cost announced at the final investment decision, in US dollars, with a capitalised lease where its source includes one.
  • Awards set against the estimate: point values, published ranges, contractor backlog values and the published range of a contractor's size word, at their low bound. Lease and operate values, third-party export lines, values that cover several projects and unpublished values are left out, so the share is a floor.
  • Final over sanction: the first sanction estimate and the final outturn, or the forecast at completion, in one currency. Each figure keeps its source's words (about, more than, within, under), from the repository's review where there is one and otherwise from the quoted words, and its money basis (money of the day or a fixed year's money), as Table A7 shows. A final whose words say it is expected is shown as a forecast at completion. The change is not computed where the source states no final cost or where the sanction estimate is itself a lower bound; a final stated as an upper bound gives a change of at most the value shown. The capital blend over the same years uses the bell-shaped spend from the sanction year to the year of the final figure.

Limits

The results are for setting the escalation allowance and its contingency on a US-dollar estimate at sanction. The indexes are US prices; equipment and yard work bought abroad meet their own markets, which is the gap Figure 3 shows in the boom years. The sanction-year windows overlap, so they are not independent samples, and the record holds two booms and two busts. The escalation is market price movement only; it holds no scope growth, schedule delay or productivity change. The project view holds only what operators, partners, regulators and contractors published: a project with no published final figure is not in Figure 5, and the US-dollar capital blend is set beside projects costed in other currencies.
