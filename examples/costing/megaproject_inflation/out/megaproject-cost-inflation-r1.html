Offshore mega-project cost inflation over time

The Deckhand Chronicles is AceEngineer's newsletter. We help build AI into everyday engineering work.

What we did

A mega project fixes its budget at sanction, then spends it over five to eight years while the prices of rigs, steel, equipment and engineering move. On 17 November 2026 Vamsee Achanta asked our team how offshore project costs have inflated over time. We set public price indexes against consumer prices over a quarter of a century. This article continues our series on costing toward mega projects.

Why it matters

  • Shows how far offshore cost indexes have outrun consumer prices, and how far each boom and bust moved them.
  • Gives the escalation a project met for each sanction year, for a median estimate and the contingency above it.
  • The same check carries to any cost index, spend profile or execution window.
  • Prompt: rebuild the index comparison and escalation range from the public files, in worldenergydata on GitHub.

What is inside

The body gives the long-run rise, the three cycles, a check against a licensed sector index and the escalation range by sanction year. Appendix A holds the data, weights, method and tables. Appendix B holds the prompt.

Offshore cost indexes rose faster than consumer prices

Eight public US price indexes, each rebased to its 2000 mean, are compared with consumer prices from 2000 to 2025. The yearly growth of each above consumer prices is shown in Figure 1.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • Consumer prices rose 87.0 %, or 2.53 % a year [1].
  • The cost of drilling oil and gas wells rose 189.4 % and steel pipe and tube 252.9 % [2][6]: 1.76 % and 2.57 % a year above consumer prices.
  • Engineering services, ship building and support activities kept pace with consumer prices, within 0.13 % a year of them [3][7][8].
  • The capital blend, seven cost indexes weighted as for an offshore development, rose 3.35 % a year, 0.79 % a year above consumer prices.

Three cycles moved costs by tens of percent

The monthly indexes against consumer prices, with the three cycles shaded, are shown in Figure 2.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • 2004 to 2008: the capital blend rose 63.9 % from 2003 to 2008, 40.1 % above consumer prices; drilling rose 139.0 % [2].
  • 2014 to 2016: the blend fell 12.2 % from 2014 to 2016; drilling fell 33.9 % from March 2014 to November 2016 [2].
  • 2021 to 2023: the blend rose 26.0 % from 2020 to 2023, only 7.0 % above consumer prices, which rose too; steel mill products rose 153.2 % from August 2020 to December 2021 [5].
  • 2026 so far: from January to October the blend averages 4.0 % above its 2025 mean, steel mill products 19.6 % and Brent crude oil 39.4 % [5][9].

Our hypothesis: the oil price sets the swing in drilling and steel costs, with drilling about a year behind, while engineering and yard fabrication costs move with consumer prices.

From 1988 to 2025, the yearly change in drilling cost follows the Brent change of the year before with a correlation of 0.56. Steel mill products follow the same year's Brent change at 0.70, ship building at 0.12 and engineering services at 0.10 [9].

Public indexes miss part of the 2008 to 2013 peak

The S&P Global (formerly IHS) Upstream Capital Costs Index is licensed; its values are public only where the press quoted them [10]–[22]. The quoted values beside the two public blends are shown in Figure 3.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • The quoted index reached 230 in the third quarter of 2008 [11]; the capital blend stood at 184.4 in that quarter.
  • In the 8 quoted values from 2008 to 2013, the quoted index sits 18.8 % to 24.7 % above the capital blend.
  • In the 5 quoted values of 2006, 2019 and 2020, it sits 0.8 % to 4.2 % below.
  • The two-index blend matches the boom years more closely, within 5.1 % below to 8.0 % above, but sits 11.1 % to 18.4 % above the quoted index in the other years.
  • No value is public for 2014 to 2018 or after the third quarter of 2020.

Our hypothesis: in a boom, global yard, vessel and subsea equipment markets add cost that US producer price indexes do not see, so an escalation built on them alone understates a boom by about a fifth.

The fifth is the 18.8 % to 24.7 % gap of the boom-year values; outside the boom the two agree within 4.2 %.

A longer execution window widens the escalation range

For each sanction year, the estimate is set in that year's money and spent over 5 or 8 years on a bell-shaped profile. The escalation above consumer prices that each project would have met is shown in Figure 4.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • 5-year window, sanction years 1997 to 2021: the median (P50, half the years met less) is 0.7 % above consumer prices; the P90 (nine in ten met less) is 11.8 %.
  • 8-year window, sanction years 1997 to 2018: P50 0.7 %, P90 22.8 %; with consumer prices included, P50 10.3 % and P90 36.2 %.
  • The worst sanction year is 2003: 29.7 % above consumer prices over 8 years, 44.2 % in all; the best is 2014, at −11.8 %.
  • On a P50 estimate, the escalation contingency to P90 is 11.1 percentage points for 5 years and 22.1 for 8 years, on top of the allowance for consumer prices.
  • Other weights and an even spend give an 8-year P90 of 19.3 % to 22.8 %; further checks are given in Appendix A.

Method

  • Annual value: the calendar-year mean of the monthly values. Rebased index: the annual value over its 2000 value, times 100.
  • Growth above consumer prices: the rebased index over the rebased consumer price index. Growth per year is compounded from 2000 to 2025.
  • Capital blend: the weighted mean of seven rebased cost indexes, with the weights of Table A2. The weights are our assumption for the capital of a deepwater development; they are not measured cost shares.
  • Cycles: the change in annual means from 2003 to 2008, 2014 to 2016 and 2020 to 2023 (Table A3). The turning months in the body are the highest and lowest monthly values in each period.
  • Escalation over a window: for sanction year s and a window of N years, the spend share of year s + k is proportional to sin(π(k + 0.5)/N); escalation is the spend-weighted index over its sanction-year value, less one. Percentiles interpolate linearly across the sanction years.
  • Correlation: Pearson correlation of the yearly percentage changes of the annual means.

Limits

The results are for setting the escalation allowance and its contingency on a US-dollar estimate at sanction. The indexes are US prices; equipment and yard work bought abroad meet their own markets, which is the gap Figure 3 shows in the boom years. The sanction-year windows overlap, so they are not independent samples, and the record holds two booms and two busts. The escalation is market price movement only; it holds no scope growth, schedule delay or productivity change.
