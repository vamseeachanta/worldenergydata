Offshore mega-project cost inflation over time

The Deckhand Chronicles is AceEngineer's newsletter. We help build AI into everyday engineering work.

What we did

A mega project fixes its budget at sanction, then spends it over several years while the prices of rigs, steel, equipment and engineering move. Public price indexes are set against consumer prices from 2000 to 2025. The final costs of existing offshore projects are set against their sanction estimates, from public records. This article continues our series on costing toward mega projects, which grows with each project read.

Why it matters

  • Study how far offshore cost indexes have outrun consumer prices, and how far each boom and bust moved them.
  • Check an estimate against the escalation met in each sanction year, and against what earlier projects finally cost.
  • Rerun the comparison with any cost index, spend profile or execution window.
  • Prompt: rebuild the index comparison, escalation range and project view from the public files, in worldenergydata on GitHub.

What is inside

The body gives the long-run rise, the three cycles, a check against a licensed sector index, the escalation range by sanction year and a first project-level view. Appendix A holds the data, method and tables. Appendix B holds the prompt.

  • 2021 to 2023: the blend rose {{T_boom3}} % from 2020 to 2023, {{T_boom3_real}} % above consumer prices; steel mill products rose {{T_steel_boom}} % from {{T_steel_boom_from}} to {{T_steel_boom_to}} [5].

Our hypothesis: the oil price sets the swing in drilling and steel costs, with drilling about a year behind (correlation {{T_r_drill}} on the previous year's Brent change [9]). Engineering and yard fabrication costs follow it more weakly and keep pace with consumer prices over the long run.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • The quoted index reached {{T_peak}} in the third quarter of 2008 [{{T_peak_ref}}]; the capital blend stood at {{T_peak_blend}} in that quarter.
  • In the {{T_boom_n}} quoted values from {{T_boom_y0}} to {{T_boom_y1}}, the quoted index sits {{T_gap_min}} % to {{T_gap_max}} % above the capital blend.
  • In the {{T_calm_n}} quoted values of 2006, 2019 and 2020, it sits {{T_calm_lo}} % to {{T_calm_hi}} % below.
  • No quoted value was found for 2014 to 2018 or after 2020; the 2013 value is set aside (Appendix A).

Our hypothesis: in a boom, global yard, vessel and subsea equipment markets add cost that US producer price indexes do not see. An escalation built on them alone would then understate a boom.

Appendix A Method and data

This appendix gives the data, the method, the long tables, the checks and the limits of the work.

Data

Seven cost indexes, the consumer price index and the Brent price were downloaded from FRED as monthly values and saved unchanged; the files and their SHA-256 are given in Table A1. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics did not publish the consumer price index for October 2025, so the 2025 value is the mean of 11 months. The project tables are read unchanged from the curated cost files {{REF_PROJ}}, each checked against its recorded SHA-256.

{{TAB_SOURCES}}

Table A1 – Series used, with retrieval time and SHA-256

Method

  • Annual value: the calendar-year mean of the monthly values. Rebased index: the annual value over its 2000 value, times 100.
  • Growth above consumer prices: the rebased index over the rebased consumer price index. Growth per year is compounded from 2000 to 2025.
  • Capital blend: the weighted mean of seven rebased cost indexes, with the weights of Table A2. The weights are our assumption for the capital of a deepwater development; they are not measured cost shares.
  • Cycles: the change in annual means from 2003 to 2008, 2014 to 2016 and 2020 to 2023 (Table A3). The turning months in the body are the highest and lowest monthly values searched from 2012 to 2018 for drilling and from 2020 to 2024 for steel mill products.
  • Escalation over a window: for sanction year s and a window of N years, the spend share of year s + k is proportional to sin(π(k + 0.5)/N); escalation is the spend-weighted index over its sanction-year value, less one. Percentiles interpolate linearly across the sanction years. The 5-year and 8-year windows and the bell-shaped spend profile are our assumptions; Table A4 checks an even spend.
  • Correlation: Pearson correlation of the yearly percentage changes of the annual means.

Project costs

A project's cost is assembled from three kinds of public record, and each value is used as its source states it:

  • Sources that count: the operator's or a partner's release, annual report, investor presentation or filing; a regulator's document; and a contractor's own release, for the figure each states. A value read in the trade press, or in a later report that quotes the operator, and a value derived rather than stated, are used only where no such source was found. Each is marked † and is ASSUMED - to be confirmed by {{T_confirm_by}} against the operator's or the regulator's own document.
  • Estimate at sanction: the gross capital cost announced at the final investment decision, in US dollars, with a capitalised lease where its source includes one.
  • Awards set against the estimate: point values, published ranges, contractor backlog values and the published range of a contractor's size word, at their low bound. Lease and operate values, third-party export lines, values that cover several projects and unpublished values are left out, so the share is a floor.
  • Final over sanction: the first sanction estimate and the final outturn, or the forecast at completion, in one currency. Each figure keeps its source's words (about, more than, within) and its money basis (money of the day or a fixed year's money), as Table A7 shows. The change is not computed where the source states no final cost or where the sanction estimate is itself a lower bound; a final stated as an upper bound gives a change of at most the value shown. The capital blend over the same years uses the bell-shaped spend from the sanction year to the year of the final figure.

Limits

The results are for setting the escalation allowance and its contingency on a US-dollar estimate at sanction. The indexes are US prices; equipment and yard work bought abroad meet their own markets, which is the gap Figure 3 shows in the boom years. The sanction-year windows overlap, so they are not independent samples, and the record holds two booms and two busts. The escalation is market price movement only; it holds no scope growth, schedule delay or productivity change. The project view holds only what operators, partners, regulators and contractors published: a project with no published final figure is not in Figure 5, and the US-dollar capital blend is set beside projects costed in other currencies.
