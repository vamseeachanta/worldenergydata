Gulf of Mexico well permits: a public US data file

The Deckhand Chronicles is AceEngineer's newsletter. We help build AI into everyday engineering work.

What we did

Planning work over the life of a well starts from what was done before. The US offshore safety regulator publishes every application for a permit to drill in federal waters as a free data file. This first article of our series on the life of Gulf of America wells counts new-well permits since 2005, by rig type and water depth.

Why it matters

  • See where and how new wells are now drilled, and how the count moved with the oil price.
  • Anyone can rerun the counts on the latest file and add their own questions.
  • The counting rules and reusable prompts to rerun the counts are in our public repository.

Prompt: count new-well permits by year, rig type and water depth from the public permit file: worldenergydata on GitHub.

What is inside

It holds a map of new wells by water depth, year by year, with their rolling averages beside the oil price, and the permits by rig type and depth band. Links to the reusable prompts and the counting rules are in Appendices A and B.

1 Public permit data

Public data shows how new-well permits in US federal waters of the Gulf of America (formerly the Gulf of Mexico) have changed since 2005. The counts come from the Application for Permit to Drill (APD) raw data of the US Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE). The map uses its borehole raw data; the oil price is the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) WTI spot price. The public data set is two BSEE raw data files (permits to drill, boreholes) and the EIA WTI spot price.

Some of the observations are as follows:

  • @@MAP_OBSERVATION@@ (Figure 1a).
  • @@TREND_OBSERVATION@@ (Figure 1b).
  • Each year now has far fewer permits than in the mid-2000s (Figure 2).
  • Most permits once named a jack-up rig; since 2015, most name a drillship (Figure 2).
  • Permits in less than 100 m fell the most. Those in 500 m or more changed far less; since 2015, they are most permits (Figure 4).

Figure 1a shows where new wells started, coloured by water depth, in five-year windows. The map moves through every window from 2005 to 2009 to 2021 to 2025, one year at a time, until you click it; then the slider picks a window and the legend turns each water-depth band on or off. Figure 1b counts the same wells each year, with a three-year rolling average for each band beside the yearly WTI oil price; the shaded years are the window on the map, and clicking a year moves the map to the window that ends in it.

The raw data page of the BSEE Data Center lists the files used here, and files on platform structures, pipelines, production and offshore statistics by water depth.

2 Well permits by rig type and water depth

The permit file holds each application for a permit to drill in US federal waters. Each record gives:

  • The permit type.
  • The requested spud date: the date drilling is to start.
  • The water depth.
  • The rig name and rig type.

The counting rules are:

  • Count each well once, at its first New Well permit in the Gulf of Mexico region.
  • Use the requested spud year and the rig type named on that permit.

From 2005 to 2026, 2,895 wells are counted this way. The rule leaves out each well's later permits, and the 10 wells whose first New Well permit gives no requested spud date. The year 2026 runs only to the file of 2 November 2026.

2.1 Rig type

The counting script names six rig types:

  • Jack-up.
  • Drillship.
  • Semisubmersible.
  • DP semisubmersible: dynamically positioned, held on station by its thrusters.
  • Platform rig.
  • Submersible.

Barge, barge rig and well intervention vessel (19 permits) are grouped as other. Figure 2 shows the new-well permits each year by rig type. Figure 3 shows the shares in 2005, 2015 and 2025, one full year per decade.

2.2 Water depth

Every permit counted records a water depth. The file gives it in feet; this page gives it in metres (1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly, NIST SP 811, Appendix B.8). Figure 4 shows the new-well permits each year in four water-depth bands: under 100 m, 100 to 500 m, 500 to 1,500 m, and 1,500 m and deeper. A depth on a band edge is counted in the deeper band. Figure 5 shows the shares in the same three years.

3 Outlook and open points

3.1 Our hypotheses

Our hypothesis: with few new-well permits now naming a jack-up (Figure 2), jack-up work in US federal Gulf of Mexico waters will turn further from new wells to plugging and abandonment.

Our hypothesis: with deep-water permits falling far less than shallow-water permits (Figure 4), new-well engineering in these waters will centre further on deep-water drilling, risers and subsea equipment.

3.2 Open points

  • Each count takes the rig type named in a well's first permit. How often a different type of rig drilled the well is open.
  • Figure 1b sets the oil price beside the well counts only. How much of each change follows the price, and how much follows other events such as changes in regulation, is open.
